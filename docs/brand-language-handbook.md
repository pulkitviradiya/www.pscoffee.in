# P.S. Coffee Brand Language Handbook

2026-09-19 · @Someone

For the marketing, branding, social and creative teams. This is the rulebook and the reasoning behind it. The exact approved wording for every surface lives in the Master Copy System v4; this document tells you how to write anything that document does not already cover.

## How to use this handbook

### Who it is for

Anyone who writes a word that a customer will read. Campaign copy, captions, packaging, in-store signage, notifications, ads, decks, scripts, event collateral, influencer briefs, job posts.

If you are briefing an agency, a freelancer or a production partner, send them this document. Do not paraphrase it for them. Paraphrased brand rules turn into approximate brand rules within two rounds.

### Which document to reach for

| You need | Go to |
| --- | --- |
| The exact approved words for a website page, cup, email or notification | Master Copy System v4 |
| To write something new that the Copy System does not cover | This handbook |
| Colours, type, layout, imagery | Brand Design Kit |

Where this handbook and the Design Kit disagree on anything to do with words, this handbook wins.

### The precedence order

Every brand accumulates good ideas that are wrong for it. When two things conflict, the lower number wins. This settles most arguments without a meeting.

1. **Speed at the counter.** A grab-and-go brand that queues is not this brand.
2. **Price legibility.** The number is friendly, so it is never hidden.
3. **Clarity for a first-timer.** Someone who has never been here orders correctly without asking.
4. **Voice consistency.** The same brand wrote every surface.
5. **Cleverness.** Last, always.

A line that wins on 5 and loses on 1 to 4 gets cut, however much anyone likes it. This applies to your favourite line too.

### What is locked and what is live

**Locked.** The positioning, the two registers, the menu structure, the P.S. density rules, the pod naming system, the quality claim, the price doctrine. These change only for a real reason, through the brand owner, not because someone had a better idea in a review.

**Live.** P.S. lines, the rotating slate copy, per-site salutations, captions, notification phrasing, local words. These are supposed to keep growing. Add to them. A living layer that changes is the system working.

### What this document does not cover

Three areas are deliberately incomplete and no one should write copy for them yet: subscription and the Pass, the founder story, and exterior facade signage. If a brief asks for any of these, escalate rather than invent.

## The core idea: the letter and the postscript

A postscript is short, it comes after the main thing, and it is the part people actually read. That is a precise description of what a grab-and-go coffee should be.

**The day is the letter. The coffee is the P.S.**

Everything in this handbook follows from that sentence. If you understand it, you can write for this brand without memorising the rest.

### Why it works as a business idea and not just a name

A postscript is brief by definition. It does not interrupt the letter, it completes it. That is the same promise as ninety seconds in a lobby: we are not asking for your afternoon, we are asking for the gap between two things you were already doing.

It is also chosen. Nobody is obliged to write a postscript. You add it because something still mattered. That is the difference between a habit and a routine, and it is why the brand talks about being chosen rather than being convenient.

### The two halves, and where each one lives

| The letter | The P.S. |
| --- | --- |
| The day, the building, the routine | The coffee, the interruption |
| The printed board, four drinks, never changes | The rotating versions behind it |
| Serious register: beans, dose, price, hours | Drama register: sign-offs, captions, notifications |
| The site and its locality | The salutation written to it |

The most common mistake in the first version of this brand was using the postscript and never using the letter. A postscript with nothing to be appended to is just a decoration.

### What this means in practice

**Every Pod is addressed to something.** The window, the opening post and the site-specific copy are written to the building: *Dear Fourth Floor.* *Dear Everyone With A 9am.* That is the letter, and it is what makes Pod eleven feel written for its building rather than rolled out to it.

**Every surface ends with a P.S.** It is always last. It is never the headline. There is never more than one.

**The menu is the same structure.** The Letter is the permanent board. The P.S. is the rotating range behind it. A customer who never reads a word of brand copy still experiences the idea.

### One test you can apply to any idea

If someone proposes something, ask which half it belongs to. If the answer is neither, it probably does not belong to this brand. A brand mascot is neither. A loyalty gamification layer is neither. A rotating slate is the P.S. A site-specific opening film is the letter.

## The positioning line, and what drama means

> We take coffee very seriously, as well as the drama.

### Where this line is allowed

About page, landing page, internal decks, B2B presentations, the X bio. Nowhere else.

It is not signage, not a cup line, not a repeated tagline, not a campaign end-frame. It is a statement someone reads once and understands the brand from. Repeat it on every surface and it flattens into a slogan within a month.

Short form for internal use and staff training: *We take coffee seriously. And the drama.*

### Drama has two meanings here, and you need both

**Product drama.** Everyone else serves one version of each drink. One americano, forever. One cappuccino. We serve the original and several ways to have it, across the board, and the list rotates.

This is the commercial half. It is the thing a chain cannot match at our price point, and it is the reason a customer is still interested in month six rather than bored by March.

**Copy drama.** Our customer spends eight hours surrounded by careful, hedged, approved language. Every other surface in that building was written by a committee. We are the one that was not.

This is the brand half. It sets the volume too: dry, not loud. Loud reads as marketing, marketing reads as corporate, and corporate is the thing we are relief from.

### What drama is not

Worth stating plainly, because the word invites all three of these.

- **Not theatre at the counter.** Nobody performs at a customer while they wait. The transaction stays ninety seconds.
- **Not gossip about customers.** Observational lines never identify anyone. "Someone ordered a quad at 8:40" is fine. Naming a regular is not.
- **Not emotional excess.** No exclamation marks, no capitals for emphasis, no hype.

### The relationship between the two halves

The positioning line is a single sentence containing both, and that is deliberate. Take either half away and the brand breaks.

Serious without drama is every other specialty brand: correct, earnest, forgettable. Drama without serious is a novelty account that cannot sell a second cup.

When you review any piece of work, check both are present. A perfectly dry piece of quality copy with no personality anywhere has failed the brief just as much as a joke with no coffee in it.

## The two registers

The brand sounds like a familiar barista who knows your order and does not need to make conversation about it. Warm, brief, a little dry, never explaining itself.

It writes in two registers. The rule that holds the whole voice together: **they never mix inside one sentence.**

|  | Serious register | Drama register |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covers | Beans, roast, dose, training, price, hours, hygiene, allergens, safety, legal | Sign-offs, captions, seasonal notes, apologies, anything after the P.S. |
| Sounds like | Flat declaratives. No wordplay, no jokes, no adjectives doing emotional work. | Gossipy, brief, complicit, deadpan. |
| Sentence length | Short | Shorter |
| Who it sounds like | The person who trained the barista | The barista, to a regular, when it is quiet |

### The governing rule: dramatic content, deadpan delivery

The subject matter can be as theatrical as you like. The sentences describing it stay flat.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| Someone ordered a QUAD at 8:40am! Legend! | P.S. Someone ordered a quad at 8:40. We are monitoring the situation. |
| We're SO excited to bring you three amazing new drinks! | P.S. Three new on the slate. One of them will not survive the month. |
| Oops! We made a little mistake last week, sorry! | P.S. We over-roasted a batch last week. Binned it. Sorry about Thursday. |
| Our beans are lovingly sourced and carefully crafted | Specialty grade, every lot. Ground at every order. |

Same content each time, opposite delivery. The right-hand column is funnier, and it is the only version that survives being read three hundred times by a regular. Deadpan improves with repetition. Enthusiasm decays.

### The exclamation mark test

Before any line ships, ask: would an exclamation mark help this line?

- If adding one would **improve** it, the line is too weak. Rewrite.
- If adding one would **ruin** it, the line is right.

This single test catches most register failures.

### Worked example of the two registers in one piece

A counter card for a new variant. Both registers, cleanly separated, never blended.

> **P.S. Black No. 3**
>
> Lighter, sharper, floral. Not for a bad morning.
>
> Specialty grade, every lot. Ground at order.
>
> P.S. Here until it is not.

Line one and three are serious. Line two and four are drama. No sentence contains both.

### A note on adjectives

The serious register is allowed descriptive words about taste, because taste is factual to the drinker: lighter, sharper, floral, clean, milk-forward. It is not allowed evaluative words about the brand: lovingly, carefully, passionately, expertly. Describe the cup, never the effort behind it.

## The P.S. sign-off

The signature move of the brand. Treat P.S. as a position, not a decoration.

### The three placement rules

1. **Always last.** It is the final line on its surface. Never a headline, never mid-page.
2. **One per surface.** On a web page, read surface as page section, and cap the whole page at three.
3. **Written with both full stops.** P.S., never PS. No dash after it. `P.S. See you tomorrow.` not `P.S. — See you tomorrow.`

That second rule is what separates a signature from a gimmick. The moment P.S. appears three times on one cup it stops reading as a warm aside and becomes a pun the brand cannot stop making.

### The anchor line

> P.S. See you tomorrow.

Build on this one. It does positioning and retention at once: it assumes a habit rather than selling one, and it only makes sense from a brand that expects to be part of a routine.

Home: cups, receipts, the order-confirmed screen, the About footer. Keep it out of anything that reads as an announcement, where it lands as a slogan rather than a sign-off.

### The four tests

Every P.S. line passes all four or it does not ship.

1. Could a person say it out loud to a regular without sounding scripted?
2. Does it add something, rather than repeat what is already on the surface?
3. Is it under about ten words?
4. Would an exclamation mark help it? If yes, it is too weak.

And the long-run test that most lines fail: **does it survive being read three hundred times by the same person?** This brand is read daily, by the same people, for years. A line that is funny once and tiring by Thursday is worse than a plain one.

### The line bank by type

Aim for a mix. If every P.S. is a joke it stops being a voice and becomes a gimmick. In any given month, roughly half should be habit or location lines.

| Type | Job | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Habit** | The workhorse. Most sign-offs are these. | P.S. See you tomorrow. · P.S. Same time, same order. |
| **Location** | Wayfinding with a personality. | P.S. Floor 3, past the lift. · P.S. We are in your building now. |
| **Range** | The differentiator. Use often. | P.S. Your usual is never one of the ones that leaves. · P.S. Here until it is not. · P.S. There are currently four ways to have an americano. |
| **Gossip** | The drama register, deadpan. | P.S. The 8am crowd is faster than the 11am crowd. Not close. · P.S. Someone ordered a quad at 8:40. We are monitoring the situation. |
| **Confession** | Builds more trust than any claim. | P.S. We over-roasted a batch last week. Binned it. Sorry about Thursday. |
| **Nudge** | Timing, never pressure. | P.S. It is 3:40. This is not like you. |
| **Report** | New stock, rotation, change. | P.S. New beans this morning. · P.S. No. 3 leaves Friday. Consider yourself told. |

### One register to handle carefully

A line that comments on someone's absence can read as surveillance when it comes from software rather than a person. Keep the default observational, not personal.

- Safe: *P.S. No coffee yet today.*
- Riskier: *P.S. Everything alright?*

The first observes. The second asks about someone's life. Test the second register with a small group before it becomes a default.

### Writing new ones

You are expected to. This is a living layer. The best sources are the Pods themselves: what the queue does, what people order, what went wrong, what arrived this morning. A line that reports something real will always beat a line someone invented in a meeting.

## The initialism layer, and local language

### P.S. on objects

A second system, doing a different job from the sign-off. The two never share a surface, which is what stops either from collapsing into a joke told twice.

|  | Sign-off | Initialism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goes on | Messages | Objects and places |
| Position | Always last | Wherever the label goes |
| Job | Voice | Wayfinding |
| Cap | One per surface | Eight to ten per Pod |

**The test: would this label exist if the brand were called something else?**

If yes, the P.S. is doing real work, because the thing needed a name anyway. If no, it is the joke wearing a costume.

| Passes | Fails |
| --- | --- |
| Purush / Stri on the restroom doors | A bin labelled "P.S. Bin" |
| Pick-up Shelf on the collection counter | A wall that says "P.S. Wall" |
| Please Sort on waste segregation | A door labelled "P.S. Door" |

### The starting bank

Deliberately functional. These are labels a Pod needs anyway.

| Object | Label |
| --- | --- |
| Restrooms, as a pair, one word per door | Purush / Stri |
| Pickup counter | Pick-up Shelf |
| Order screen | Pending Sips |
| Waste segregation | Please Sort |
| Water station | Paani Station |
| Ledge seating, two stools maximum | Please Sit |
| Staff area | Private Space |
| Feedback | Politely Said |
| Morning playlist | Pehla Sur |
| Afternoon playlist | Post Shift |

Purush / Stri only works as a pair. One word per door, reading as P.S. when you stand back. A single unisex restroom needs a different device.

Past roughly ten initialisms in one Pod the eye stops registering them. The cap is real.

### Hindi and Gujarati

English, with a small number of local words for texture. The discipline is **words, not sentences.**

| Use it for | Never use it for |
| --- | --- |
| Object and place labels: Paani Station, Purush / Stri | Sign-off lines |
| Playlist names: Pehla Sur | Social captions |
| A site-specific product name | Notifications |
| A single word inside an otherwise English line | Menu board copy, pricing, allergens |

Roughly three to five local words live in a Pod. At that density they read as belonging. Past it they read as a theme.

**Never code-switch mid-sentence.** The moment the voice mixes languages inside a sentence it starts sounding like an agency's idea of a young Indian brand, which is exactly the register we are relief from.

The reason this works at all: the audience does not talk in pure English, so an all-English brand leaves warmth on the table. But they do read in it, and clarity at a counter at 8:45am is worth more than texture.

## What we claim, and what we never claim

The two areas where a well-meaning writer does the most damage. Read this section before writing anything about the coffee or the price.

### The quality claim

**The rule: claim the grade, never the species.**

| Say | Never say |
| --- | --- |
| Specialty grade, every lot. | 100% Arabica |
| Honest specialty coffee | Single-origin Arabica, or any species claim |
| Coffee · Specialty grade · Ground at order | No Robusta. No chicory. |
| Specialty grade. Every cup. | Premium, artisanal, crafted, curated, gourmet |
| Ground at every order. Not this morning. Not in bulk. Now. | Freshly brewed, barista-made, lovingly roasted |

**Why, and this matters.** The bean composition is a sourcing decision that must stay free to change. If any copy says Arabica and the blend later changes, the brand has lied on its own packaging, and honesty is the whole proposition. If any copy is anti-Robusta and the blend later includes it, the same thing happens twice over.

Robusta is a taste decision, not a quality failure. Fine robusta carries crema and body that many Indian palates prefer. The species is not what makes a cup good, which is precisely why we do not stake the quality claim on it.

**Where detail belongs.** Origin, roast profile and grind guidance go on the bean bag back label and the app detail screen, in the serious register, for the customer who went looking. Never on the board, never in a caption, never on the landing page.

**Describe what the drink does, not where it came from.**

| Snob | Ours |
| --- | --- |
| Washed Yirgacheffe, 1,900 MASL, jasmine and bergamot | P.S. Black No. 3. Lighter, sharper, floral. Not for a bad morning. |

### The price doctrine

Price operates at three levels and only two are public.

| Level | What it is | Where it lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The gap | India fell in love with specialty coffee and never built a daily price point for it. A category observation. | About page, founding narrative. Stated once. |
| The number | What a cup costs. | Menu page and Pass cards, as data. Never explained. |
| The argument | Why the format allows the number. | Investor decks and partner conversations. Never in customer copy. |

**State the gap once. Price the cup plainly. Never argue the case.**

### Why we stopped arguing about price

Three reasons, and the team should know all three, because the pricing argument is seductive and someone will propose it again.

**A brand that explains its price teaches people to evaluate it on price.** Every explanation invites a comparison. Affordability that is felt on the second visit is durable. Affordability that is argued is a position a competitor takes from you with a discount.

**It is contested ground already.** The largest grab-and-go coffee brand in the country runs on rational prices as its stated position, at scale. A smaller brand making the same argument is making the larger brand's case for them.

**It crowds out the product.** Range is what a competitor cannot copy in a quarter. Price is what they can.

### Banned pricing vocabulary

Cheap. Affordable. Budget. Discount. Value-for-money. Student logic. Bargain. Save. Deal. Offer.

Never apologise for the price. Never frame it as a bargain. State it and move on.

**Affordable, not cheap.** Those read very differently, and only one of them is a brand.

## Naming and structure

### The menu: The Letter and The P.S.

The ideology made literal. A customer who never reads a word of brand copy still experiences it.

| Layer | What it is | Where it lives | Job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The Letter** | Four drinks. P.S. Black, P.S. White, P.S. Green, P.S. Strong. Never change. | Printed board, permanent | Speed, price legibility, defaults |
| **The P.S.** | Three or four versions at a time, rotating. | Counter card, slate, app | Range, discovery, repeat interest |
| **The Usual** | The saved order. | App button, counter | Removes the decision entirely |

**Variants are numbered, never named.** P.S. Black No. 3 is sayable at a counter at 8:45am. The Midnight Rebellion is not. If someone proposes a poetic name for a drink, the answer is no, and the reason is the queue.

**The Usual is a term of art.** Same word on the app home button, the confirmation screen, the barista screen, the notification and at the counter. Never paraphrased as your saved order, your favourite, or your regular.

### Pods

Outlets are always Pods, always capitalised. Never kiosk, outlet, cafe, store, counter or branch.

Three naming layers, kept separate.

| Layer | Example | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Code | PS-04 | Internal only: POS, inventory, rota, financial model |
| Name | P.S. Coffee, Prahlad Nagar | Customers, app, maps, staff |
| Salutation | Dear Fourth Floor, | Window, opening, socials, site copy |

**Naming rule:** use the words someone would say when giving directions. Not the lease name, not the developer's branding, not an invented nickname. No cute Pod names in public: they break search and maps, and they force a customer to explain the brand before they can give a direction.

Staff say the name, never the code.

**The salutation is where the letter idea lives on the ground.** One line per site, written to the building:

- Opening window: *Dear Prahlad Nagar. We are in your lobby now.*
- Corporate: *Dear Fourth Floor,*
- Campus: *Dear Everyone With A 9am,*
- Gym: *Dear People Who Came Here To Be Better,*

Everything else about a Pod is standard, which is what makes the one personal line land.

### Placement language

An easy mistake, and it was in the first version of the copy. Not every Pod is inside a building. Some sit on the corner outside one.

| Correct | Wrong |
| --- | --- |
| In and around the places your day already runs through | Inside the buildings you already use |
| Inside your building, or the corner you pass on the way in | Always inside your building |
| Placed where your day already runs | Inside your office |

Never claim every Pod is inside. The umbrella phrase is *in and around*; the concrete version names both options.

### The scope rule for the americano

The americano is the clearest illustration of the range and it is not the scope of it. Every drink on the board has versions.

Any line that names the americano carries the whole board with it, either explicitly or by saying "each drink" instead.

- Right: *There is a right americano for a Monday and a different one for a Thursday, and the same is true of the cappuccino.*
- Wrong: the first half on its own.

A brand that only ever demonstrates range on one drink has one drink with range.

## Channel playbook: social and physical

### Social

**What the account is.** A postscript feed, not a coffee content account. Short observed lines about the site, the beans, the queue and the people.

**Standing format.** One photograph. One line. Then the P.S. Nothing else. Weekly at minimum. The restraint is the personality.

**Caption structure.** Plain observation, then the sign-off. The caption body is not the joke. The P.S. is.

> 8:41 at the Prahlad Nagar Pod. The queue cleared in ninety seconds. We timed it. P.S. Someone still checked their watch.

> The Ethiopian arrived this morning. P.S. It will not last the month.

> We over-roasted a batch on Tuesday and binned it. P.S. Sorry about Wednesday.

**What it publishes**

- Observations from the Pods, in the drama register
- Rotation reports: what landed, what is leaving
- Salutations when a new site opens
- Confessions when something goes wrong

**What never appears**

| Never | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Latte art carousels | There is no vibe to photograph. The cup is the experience. |
| Monday quotes, hustle content, "you deserve this" | Moralising. Banned by name. |
| Countdowns, teasers, "introducing" | We report, we never announce. |
| Emoji and exclamation marks | Punctuation doing the emotional work. |
| The three pillars recited in a caption | That is a press release. |
| A customer's face or name without consent | Observational never means identifying. |
| Any reference to a competitor | We are not the challenger in a scale fight. |

**Bio lines.** Instagram and LinkedIn carry the range. X carries the positioning line, because a bio is read once, which is the only context where that line repeats safely.

### Packaging and physical surfaces

**The governing rule.** One P.S. per surface. A cup sleeve is one surface with two faces, so only the front carries it.

| Surface | What goes on it |
| --- | --- |
| Cup sleeve, front | The P.S. line |
| Cup sleeve, reverse | Serious register only. Specialty grade. Ground at order. |
| Takeaway bag | Wordmark, one plain line, one P.S. on the base or gusset |
| Bean bag, front | Drink name, grade, roast date reference |
| Bean bag, back | Full serious register: origin, roast profile, grind guidance. No sign-off. |
| Receipt footer | One P.S. |
| Napkin | P.S. alone |
| Rotating slate | Header *This week*, footer *P.S. Here until it is not.* |

**Why the bag matters more than people think.** It is carried through a building by someone who bought coffee, past people who did not. It is read by strangers. Keep it plain and let the one line do the work.

**The return address plate.** Every Pod carries one, near the counter, identical format at every site:

```
P.S. COFFEE
PS-04 · Prahlad Nagar
Opened March 2027
Fourth letter.
```

A letter carries a return address. This costs almost nothing, makes a rollout feel like a correspondence rather than a franchise, and by Pod twelve regulars notice and compare them.

### Staff language

In a Pod format the staff are most of the brand experience. Consistency at the counter does more for the brand than every printed surface combined.

| Moment | Phrase |
| --- | --- |
| Greeting a regular | The usual? |
| Greeting a first-timer | What are you having? |
| Time quote | Four minutes. |
| Handing over | See you tomorrow. |
| Out of something | That is back Thursday. |
| Something went wrong | That one is on us. |
| The slate, once, unprompted | There are three on the slate today. |

**The drama is written, not performed.** Nobody delivers a P.S. line aloud unless it is one of the house phrases above. The personality lives on surfaces so the transaction can stay fast. A barista performing the brand at a queue of eleven people breaks the first rule in the precedence order.

## Channel playbook: app, email, B2B and hiring

### App and notifications

The reminder is the closest fit between the name and the product. A nudge about a coffee someone missed is literally a postscript.

**Frequency rules do not bend for personality.** These are operational, not creative.

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| One notification a day, maximum | A habit reminder that fires twice is an irritation |
| Timed to the person's own pattern, not a fixed hour | The reminder arrives when they usually order, which is a different time for every user |
| Never attach a discount to a reminder | A missed day that produces a coupon teaches people to skip days |
| A lapsed-user message goes out once, then stops | Two is a nudge. Five is a brand that cannot take a hint. |

**Tone is where the drama lives.** Same slot, better line.

| Flat | Ours |
| --- | --- |
| No coffee yet today | P.S. It is 3:40. This is not like you. |
| New drinks available! | P.S. Something new on the slate at your Pod. |
| Don't miss out, ends Friday | P.S. No. 3 leaves Friday. Consider yourself told. |

That gets personality without buying extra airtime. Push is the one surface where more drama directly costs uninstalls.

**In-app copy is mostly serious register.** Short, functional, unambiguous. The drama sits in the sign-off and the slate copy only.

### Email

Every email is signed *The P.S. team* and closes with one P.S. line. No email leaves without one.

Bodies stay short. Four emails a year is the stated promise on the signup form, and it should stay true.

**The highest-value email in the system is the one admitting a mistake.** It is also the one nobody will want to send. Send it anyway.

> **Subject:** P.S. We got one wrong.
>
> We over-roasted a batch last week and served it for a day and a half before we caught it. If your coffee tasted flat on Tuesday or Wednesday, that is why. It has been binned and the next one is on us. Nothing else to add. — The P.S. team.
>
> P.S. We would rather tell you than hope you did not notice.

Nothing else the brand can publish buys as much trust as this, and it only works if it is rare and true.

### B2B and partner material

This is the one place *P.S. = Pretty Serious* belongs, and the one audience that wants more operational detail rather than less.

**The four arguments, in order.** Facilities managers are comparing you to a vending contract. Lead with what removes work from them.

1. No setup complexity. We bring the counter, the barista and the beans.
2. Real coffee in the cup. Specialty grade, documented per lot.
3. Something new every few weeks. The core four never change, what sits behind them does.
4. Built for daily, not occasional.

Argument three is the strongest one the new positioning gives you, and it is worth leading with in the enquiry conversation. The difference between an amenity people use and one they get bored of by March is a commercial argument, not a brand one.

**Commercials belong in the conversation, not on the page.** No numbers on the partners page.

### Hiring and internal

The voice does not change for recruitment. Dry, direct, no ping-pong tables.

One value that the creative team should understand because it shapes everything else: **curiosity about the cup.** Four drinks stay the same forever and everything else changes every few weeks. A barista who finds that tedious is the wrong hire, and a marketer who finds it inconvenient is too.

### Events and collaborations

Same voice, different address. The one rule specific to this channel: we partner with brands that share the anti-pretense position, and we say so plainly rather than talking about synergy or shared values.

*P.S. Tell us what you are building. We will tell you honestly whether we fit.*

## The do and do not list

Print this page. Pin it above the desk. Everything above is the reasoning; this is the operating instruction.

### Always

- Short sentences. One idea per line.
- Address the reader as "you", one person, never a demographic.
- End the surface with a P.S. line. One only, always last.
- Write P.S. with both full stops, no dash after it.
- Call outlets Pods, always capitalised.
- Claim the grade: specialty grade, every lot.
- State the price plainly where it appears, and nowhere else.
- Describe what the drink does, not where it came from.
- Carry the whole board when you mention the americano.
- Report what happened. Let the fact be the interesting thing.
- Keep the two registers in separate sentences.
- Read it out loud before you send it.

### Never

| Never | Instead |
| --- | --- |
| Introducing, launching, unveiling, now available | Report it. "New beans this morning." |
| We are excited, proud to, thrilled | Say the thing. The excitement is not the news. |
| Crafted, artisanal, curated, premium, gourmet, journey | Specialty grade. Ground at order. |
| 100% Arabica, or any species claim | Specialty grade, every lot. |
| No Robusta, no chicory | Say nothing about species at all. |
| Cheap, affordable, budget, discount, value-for-money | Say nothing about price outside the menu. |
| Community, family, tribe | You. One person. |
| You deserve this break, fuel your hustle | Nothing. Moralising is banned outright. |
| Exclamation marks and emoji in brand copy | A flatter sentence. |
| Third person about the brand | We. |
| Explaining the postscript joke | Trust it. It works or it does not. |
| Apologising for grab-and-go | We built it on purpose. |
| Naming or implying a competitor | Nothing. We do not enter that fight. |
| Invented drink names | Numbers. P.S. Black No. 3. |
| A product description over two lines | Cut it. |
| Hinglish sentences | Hinglish words, in labels only. |
| A customer's face or name without consent | The observation without the identity. |

### The five checks before anything ships

1. **Which register is it in?** If one sentence mixes serious and drama, rewrite.
2. **Is there already a P.S. on this surface?** If yes, this line is plain.
3. **Would an exclamation mark help it?** If yes, the line is too weak.
4. **Does it survive three hundred readings?** This brand is read daily for years.
5. **Does it pass the precedence order?** Speed, price legibility, first-timer clarity, voice, then cleverness.

### The read-aloud test

The single most useful thing in this document.

> Would a sharp, warm, no-nonsense person say this to a regular they know? If yes, publish. If it sounds like a marketing department wrote it, rewrite.

If a line makes you slightly self-conscious reading it out, it is wrong. That feeling is accurate.

## Worked examples

Real rewrites, with the reasoning. Most of the before-column lines are from the previous version of the brand, which means they were written by competent people following the old rules. That is the point: the problem is usually the brief, not the writer.

### 1. A product announcement

**Before:** We're excited to introduce our brand new seasonal range! Three amazing new drinks, available now for a limited time only. Don't miss out!

**After:** Three new on the slate from today. No. 3 goes to make room. **P.S. It will not last the month.**

*What changed.* Announcing became reporting. Two exclamation marks gone. "Limited time only" and "don't miss out" are urgency devices; the flat statement that something is leaving does the same job with more credibility. The drama sits in the P.S., not the body.

### 2. A quality claim

**Before:** Every cup is made with 100% Arabica beans, lovingly sourced and expertly crafted by our passionate baristas.

**After:** Specialty grade, every lot. Ground at every order, never in bulk. Pulled by someone trained to do it.

*What changed.* The species claim is gone, because the blend may change and honesty is the proposition. Lovingly, expertly and passionate are evaluative words about our effort; they are replaced by facts about the cup. "Trained to do it" is a claim a customer can verify by watching.

### 3. A price message

**Before:** Specialty coffee doesn't have to break the bank. At just ₹99, we're bringing you cafe quality at a fraction of the price.

**After:** \[Nothing. This piece should not exist.\]

*What changed.* Everything. The number belongs on the menu, not in a campaign. "Break the bank" and "fraction of the price" are discount vocabulary. If the brief was to communicate value, the correct answer is a piece about the range, because that is what makes the price feel like a good deal without anyone claiming it is.

### 4. A student-audience post

**Before:** Specialty coffee at student logic. Your thesis won't write itself, but this might help!

**After:** Specialty coffee between lectures, in the building you are already late for.

*What changed.* "Student logic" is a cheapness frame dressed as a joke, and it is faintly patronising. The rewrite keeps the audience specificity and moves it from their budget to their day. The exclamation mark goes.

### 5. A lapsed-customer notification

**Before:** We miss you! Here's 20% off your next coffee to welcome you back.

**After:** P.S. Still here when you want us.

*What changed.* The discount is removed, because attaching money to a missed day teaches people that skipping is profitable. "We miss you" is emotional assertion; the rewrite observes without asking anything.

### 6. An opening post for a new Pod

**Before:** Big news! P.S. Coffee is now open at Venus Stratum! Come visit us and grab your first cup!

**After:** Dear Venus Stratum. We are in your lobby now. **P.S. Tower B, ground floor, past the lift.**

*What changed.* The letter device does the work three exclamation marks were doing. It is addressed to the building rather than shouted at an audience, which is the whole naming system paying off. The P.S. is wayfinding, which is more useful than an invitation.

### 7. A mistake

**Before:** Oops! We had a small hiccup with one of our batches last week. Thanks for bearing with us!

**After:** We over-roasted a batch last week and served it for a day and a half before we caught it. It has been binned and the next one is on us. **P.S. We would rather tell you than hope you did not notice.**

*What changed.* "Oops" and "small hiccup" minimise, and minimising is what customers are watching for. The rewrite names the failure, the duration, the remedy. This is the highest-trust piece the brand can publish and it only works at full honesty.

### 8. A B2B enquiry follow-up

**Before:** Thank you for your interest in partnering with P.S. Coffee. We're passionate about bringing premium coffee experiences to workplaces across India.

**After:** Thanks for getting in touch. The short version: 50 to 100 sq ft, we bring the counter, the barista and the beans, and your people stop leaving the building for coffee. **P.S. Pretty Serious about this.**

*What changed.* Passionate and premium both banned. "Coffee experiences" is nothing. The rewrite leads with what removes work from the reader, which is what a facilities manager is actually buying.

### 9. A caption that is technically fine but has no drama

**Before:** Our cold brew steeps for twelve hours. Smooth, low acid, and ready when you are.

**After:** Our cold brew steeps for twelve hours. **P.S. Somebody has to start it the day before. That somebody is Rina.**

*What changed.* Nothing was wrong with the original, which is why this example matters. It was just flat. The rewrite keeps the serious line intact and adds a P.S. that reports something real. A correct piece with no personality has still missed the brief.

## Common failure modes

Nine ways this voice goes wrong, how to spot each one in a draft, and the fix. Use this list in reviews.

### 1. Register bleed

**Symptom.** A single sentence carries both a fact and a joke. *"We grind fresh at every order because life's too short for stale coffee!"*

**Why it happens.** It feels like efficiency, getting information and personality into one line.

**Fix.** Split it. Fact, full stop. Then the P.S.

### 2. Postscript inflation

**Symptom.** Three P.S. lines on one cup. Or a P.S. as a headline. Or a page with six.

**Why it happens.** The device works, so more feels better.

**Fix.** One per surface, always last, three per page maximum. If a surface already has one, everything else on it is plain.

### 3. The price creeping back

**Symptom.** Any campaign built around value, savings, or what a cafe charges. Often arrives disguised as a comparison rather than a claim.

**Why it happens.** It is the easiest story to tell and it tests well in a room.

**Fix.** Ask which of the three price levels it belongs to. If it is level three, it goes in the investor deck, not the campaign. Then ask what the piece would be about if price were off the table. That answer is usually the better campaign.

### 4. Species drift

**Symptom.** Arabica appears anywhere. Or an anti-Robusta line. Or an origin detail on a headline surface.

**Why it happens.** It is concrete and it sounds like quality, and the previous brand book said to use it.

**Fix.** Specialty grade, every lot. Detail goes to the bean bag back or the app detail screen.

### 5. Announcement voice

**Symptom.** Introducing, launching, we are excited, now available, don't miss out, limited time.

**Why it happens.** Every template on the internet is written this way.

**Fix.** Report it instead. What happened, plainly, in the past or present tense. The news is the thing, not our feelings about the thing.

### 6. Earnest but empty

**Symptom.** Everything is correct. Nothing has any personality. No P.S. that does more than restate the headline.

**Why it happens.** The do-not list is long, so a cautious writer removes everything rather than risking a mistake.

**Fix.** This is a failure, not a safe default. If a piece has no drama in it anywhere, it has missed half the positioning. Go back to the Pod and find something real to report.

### 7. Cute for cute's sake

**Symptom.** A pun that does not carry information. A P.S. Wall. A drink called The Midnight Rebellion.

**Why it happens.** The brand rewards wordplay, so wordplay starts arriving without a job.

**Fix.** Apply the initialism test: would this exist if the brand were called something else? And the precedence order: cleverness is fifth.

### 8. Surveillance tone

**Symptom.** Copy that knows too much about someone's day. *"You skipped Tuesday. Everything alright?"*

**Why it happens.** Personalisation data makes it possible, and warmth is the intent.

**Fix.** Observe, do not enquire. *P.S. No coffee yet today.* The brand notices. It does not ask about your life.

### 9. Competitor gravity

**Symptom.** Copy that positions against another grab-and-go brand, by name or by obvious description. Often arrives as "unlike some places".

**Why it happens.** There is a bigger competitor and it is tempting to take them on.

**Fix.** Cut it. Anti-cafe and anti-machine copy is fine, because those are formats. Anything aimed at a named rival makes us the challenger in a scale fight we do not win. Compete on range, silently.

### The review question that catches most of these

Before approving anything, ask the writer: **which rule made you write it this way?**

If they can name the rule, the work is usually right even when it surprises you. If the answer is "it felt right", check it against the five checks. The point of the system is that good work is explicable, not lucky.

## Quick reference

The whole system on one screen. If you read nothing else, read this.

### The idea

The day is the letter. The coffee is the P.S.

### The positioning line

*We take coffee very seriously, as well as the drama.* About page, landing page, internal and B2B decks, X bio. Nowhere else.

| Product drama | Copy drama |
| --- | --- |

### The voice in one rule

**Dramatic content, deadpan delivery.** The two registers never mix inside one sentence.

### The five non-negotiables

1. One P.S. per surface, always last, three per page maximum.
2. Specialty grade, every lot. Never a species claim.
3. Price appears on the menu as data, nowhere else, never argued.
4. No exclamation marks or emoji in brand copy.
5. Never mention or imply a competitor.

### The precedence order

Speed → price legibility → first-timer clarity → voice consistency → cleverness.

### The four tests for a P.S. line

Sayable out loud · adds something · under ten words · would be ruined by an exclamation mark.
