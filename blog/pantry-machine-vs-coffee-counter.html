The pantry machine and the counter: an honest comparison.

By P.S. Coffee Updated September 2026 4 min read

The short version

Most offices already have a coffee machine sitting in the pantry, and it usually got there for reasons that made sense at the time. This is a comparison of two formats, not an argument that one of them is wrong. No P.S. Pod has opened yet, so what follows describes the format we are building.

The Pod · 01

What the pantry machine is actually for.

A pantry machine solves a specific problem well: it is always on. No opening hours, no staffing, no queue at 9am when half the floor wants a cup at once. Someone presses a button at midnight during a release and it works exactly the same as it did at 9am. There is no vendor relationship to manage day to day, no rota, no one calling in sick. Maintenance is predictable too, a quarterly service call rather than an ongoing relationship to run.

For a building that needs coffee available and needs it to require nothing from facilities beyond restocking, a machine is a genuinely sound answer, and the person who signed that contract solved a real problem. It also scales down cleanly. A twelve-person office does not need a staffed counter. A machine does the job at a size where anything else would be overbuilt.

The Pod · 02

What it cannot do.

The honest limits of a pantry machine are mechanical, not a matter of taste. It cannot grind coffee at the moment of order, because the mechanism is built around a hopper or a pod, held for hours or days before it is used. It cannot pull an espresso shot the way a trained hand can, adjusting for the bean in front of it rather than running a fixed cycle. It offers one version of each drink, because building in range means building in complexity a machine is not designed to carry. And it does not change. The drink on offer in March is the same drink on offer in November, because a machine has no slate to rotate.

None of this is a design flaw. It is what the format is built for, and it is not built for range or freshness. It is built for consistency and uptime, which is a different job entirely.

The Pod · 03

What a counter costs the building.

A counter asks more of a building than a machine does, and it is worth saying plainly. It needs space, even a small footprint, in a spot with some footfall and some visibility, not a back corner nobody walks past. It usually needs power and a water point sorted properly rather than run off an extension cable, which is a facilities conversation a machine never requires.

It also needs a vendor relationship that is more than a service contract: someone shows up, someone runs it, someone is a presence on the floor rather than an appliance against a wall. Facilities has to manage that relationship the way it manages any other on-site service, not the way it manages a machine that gets serviced quarterly and otherwise left alone. That is a real, ongoing cost, and a fair comparison says so rather than pretending a counter is free once it exists.

The Pod · 04

Where each one wins.

A machine wins outright in a few clear cases: small headcount, where a counter would sit underused most of the day; a site that runs 24 hours, where staffing every shift is not realistic; a low-footfall floor where the traffic to justify a counter simply is not there. In those settings a machine is not a compromise, it is the right call, and a counter would be the wrong one.

A counter wins where the floor is large enough and busy enough that a proper cup, made to order, changes how the day actually runs for the people on it, and where the building can support a person and a footprint rather than only an appliance. Some buildings run both formats side by side, a machine for the quiet hours and a counter for the peak, and that split is often the most honest answer of all.

The Pod · 05

What we actually built, and who it is for.

A P.S. Pod is built for the counter case. A small footprint, specialty-grade beans ground at every order, a person running it, and a range that can change instead of staying fixed. It is not a bigger version of a pantry machine, and it is not trying to be one. It is a different answer, built for a floor where the volume and the footprint make it worth doing properly.

If that is not your building, a machine is not a lesser choice. It is the correct one for a different set of conditions, and it will likely stay that way regardless of what we build. More on the format in coffee for offices.

P.S. We are not here to replace the machine that is already working. We are here for the buildings where it is not.

Frequently asked questions

What is the alternative to a vending machine for office coffee?+
A staffed coffee counter is the main alternative, trading the machine's always-on convenience for a made-to-order cup with more range. It is not a universal upgrade. For small headcount, 24-hour sites or low-footfall floors, a machine usually remains the better answer, so the right choice depends on the building, not on picking the newer format by default.
Is an office coffee machine or a staffed counter better?+
Neither is better in every case. A machine wins on uptime, low staffing needs and predictable maintenance. A counter wins on freshness, range and a proper cup made at the moment of order, but only where footfall and floor size justify the footprint and the ongoing vendor relationship it needs.
Does an office coffee counter need a kitchen?+
No. A counter needs a small footprint, proper power and a water point, and some visibility on the floor, but not a kitchen or an exhaust system. It is closer in scale to a compact service point than to a cafe, though it does need more building support than a pantry machine.
What does a building have to provide for a coffee counter?+
Space with reasonable footfall, power and water run to the spot rather than improvised, and an ongoing vendor relationship rather than a one-off installation. Facilities ends up managing it the way it manages any on-site service, checking in regularly, rather than the way it manages a machine serviced quarterly and left alone.
When is a machine still the right answer?+
When headcount is small, when the site runs 24 hours and staffing every shift is not realistic, or when footfall on a floor is genuinely low. In those cases a machine is not a compromise, it is the correct call, and some buildings reasonably run a machine and a counter side by side for different hours.
