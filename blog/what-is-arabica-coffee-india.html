The coffee standard behind the cup.

The quick answer

Arabica coffee (Coffea arabica) is one of the two main species of coffee bean, generally grown at higher altitude and known for a smoother, more complex cup than its counterpart, Robusta. The name on a bag tells you the species, not the quality. Arabica is close to a prerequisite for specialty-grade coffee, but it is not a guarantee of it, which is why P.S. Coffee's quality claim is specialty grade, every lot, rather than a species claim.

Key takeaways

Arabica and Robusta are the two main coffee species, and they taste different because of how and where each is grown.

Arabica is typically grown at higher altitude than Robusta, which is part of what gives it more flavour complexity.

Arabica generally tastes smoother and sweeter, with fruit, chocolate and nutty notes; Robusta is stronger and more bitter, which suits different uses.

Indian Arabica from Chikmagalur and Coorg is shade-grown alongside cardamom and pepper for distinctive spice notes.

P . S .
Section 01 · Species

Arabica vs Robusta the practical difference in your cup

Robusta is hardier to grow and produces a thicker crema, which is part of why it is common in instant coffee and many commercial espresso blends. Arabica is harder to grow and tends to produce a smoother, more complex cup. Neither is a quality failure on its own; they are different species suited to different jobs.

When you taste notes of chocolate, citrus or nuts in a cup, that is usually Arabica's chemistry at work. A flatter, more bitter cup is usually Robusta, doing what it is designed to do: dependable crema at lower cost and higher volume.

P . S .
Section 02 · Origin

India grows some of the world's best Arabica

Indian Arabica from Chikmagalur in Karnataka and Coorg is shade-grown alongside spices like cardamom and pepper. This intercropping adds subtle spice notes that are distinctive to Indian-origin coffee.

India exports a meaningful share of its premium Arabica to European specialty roasters, while the everyday Indian cup is more often a Robusta-heavy blend. The best of what is grown here does not always stay here.

Specialty grade, every lot. That is the standard P.S. Coffee holds to, documented per lot rather than tied to a single species, because the bean composition is a sourcing decision that has to stay free to change as harvests do.

P . S .
Section 03 · The claim we make

Why the species alone does not settle it

Most QSR coffee in India blends Arabica with cheaper Robusta to manage the cost per cup. That is a legitimate commercial choice for a mass-market brand, not something we are arguing against here.

P.S. Coffee's own claim is deliberately different: specialty grade, every lot, documented by how the coffee scores when it is tasted and how few defects the lot carries. That is a quality claim, not a species claim, and it can survive a sourcing change in a way a species promise cannot.

P.S. The grade is the promise. Not the species.

Frequently asked questions

What does specialty grade mean?+
It is a grading standard for the cup itself, based on how the coffee scores when it is tasted and how few defects the lot carries. It describes quality rather than plant species, which is why we talk about the grade rather than what sits in the blend.
Will P.S. Coffee say which beans are in the cup?+
The grade, yes: specialty grade, documented per lot. The blend itself is a sourcing decision that has to stay free to change as harvests change, so the promise is not built on a species. The standard in the cup is the part we hold to.
